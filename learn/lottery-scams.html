Lottery scams to avoid

A real lottery prize is claimed from the lottery that sold the ticket. A message that arrives from a stranger, telling you that you won and that you must pay before you can collect, is a scam. This page is about that difference. LotteryMigo does not sell tickets, does not validate tickets, and will not contact you to say that you won.

How a real prize is checked

You compare the ticket with the drawing it was bought for. The numbers are on the selling lottery’s site, on powerball.com or megamillions.com, and in the draw files this site republishes from NY Open Data. The ticket, not a screenshot of a website, is what the lottery pays on. Small prizes are often paid by the retailer. Larger prizes are claimed at the lottery, sometimes by appointment, with identification. The lottery’s own site states the threshold and the deadline.

Mega Millions says prizes are redeemed in the jurisdiction that sold the ticket, and that the time allowed to claim runs from 90 days to one year depending on that jurisdiction. Powerball claim rules are local in the same way. A caller who says there is a single national claim desk, and that it needs your bank login, is not describing either game.

If you did buy a ticket and the numbers match, go to the lottery. Do not start by handing the ticket to someone who offered to “file” it for a percentage. LotteryMigo cannot file it either. Email to info@lotterymigo.com is for questions about this website. Mail that asks us to verify a photographed ticket will not get a validation back, because we do not have the ticket and we are not the lottery.

The pattern of a prize scam

The Federal Trade Commission’s consumer advice on fake prize, sweepstakes, and lottery scams describes a stable pattern. Someone contacts you by phone, text, email, mail, or a social app and says you won. Then they ask you to pay, or to hand over a bank account, a card number, or a government identification number, before they will release the prize. The fee is given a boring name: taxes, shipping, processing, insurance, or customs. Paying it does not produce a prize.

A second version asks you to pay to improve your chance of winning, or to stay eligible. Chance drawings do not sell odds upgrades over the phone. A third version rushes you. The deadline is tonight, the agent is not allowed to call twice, and a wire, a gift card, a crypto transfer, or a payment app is the only accepted method. Those payment methods are hard to reverse. That is why they show up in these scripts.

Scammers borrow real names. They mention Powerball, Mega Millions, the FTC, a state lottery, or a publisher that runs a genuine sweepstakes. The borrowed name is the reason to look up the organization’s number yourself and call that number, rather than the number in the message. They also send a check and ask you to send part of it back. The FTC’s guidance on fake checks explains the stall: a bank can make the funds look available and discover the check is counterfeit afterward. The money you sent is gone.

Foreign-lottery pitches are a related script. The FTC notes that playing a foreign lottery is illegal for people in the United States, and that responding to the pitch leads to more pitches. Mega Millions’ own FAQ says the game is not sold outside the United States and is not affiliated with companies that offer tickets worldwide. A website that will buy “your” U.S. lottery tickets from overseas is not a claim path.

What LotteryMigo will never ask

This site shows statistics, guides, and a number generator. It does not have a prize department. No page here will ask you to send a fee, a photo of a signed ticket, a Social Security number, or a one-time code. The Buy Me a Coffee link in the header, when it is shown, is a voluntary tip. It does not enter a drawing, buy a lottery ticket, or speed up a claim. The Android and iOS app does not include that link.

Ads on the website are labeled as ads when a slot is filled. An advertisement is not a message from LotteryMigo saying you won. If an ad or a comment under an ad says you must pay a processing fee to a clerk, close it and report it.

If you already paid or replied

Stop sending money. If you shared a bank or card number, call the bank or the card issuer. If you shared a government identification number or a password, the FTC’s IdentityTheft.gov walks through the credit and account steps. Report the approach itself at ReportFraud.ftc.gov. The FTC also points people to their state attorney general, a local consumer-protection office, and, for a scam that arrived by mail, the U.S. Postal Inspection Service.

Keep the message, including the payment address and any names used. Do not keep negotiating with the sender. A second message that offers to refund the fee, for a further fee, is the same scam.

A short checklist

  • You can only win a drawing you entered, with a ticket from a participating lottery.
  • You do not pay a stranger’s “tax” or “release fee” to receive a prize. Taxes on a real prize are handled through the lottery’s claim process and your tax return. See the payout and tax page for the federal rules, which still require the real ticket.
  • Look up the lottery’s phone number on its own website. Use that number.
  • Gift cards, wire transfers, and cryptocurrency are a signal to stop, not a payment method the lotteries use to hand you a jackpot.
  • LotteryMigo’s results pages can help you read a draw. They cannot certify a winner.
