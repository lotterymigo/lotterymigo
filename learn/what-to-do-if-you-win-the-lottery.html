What to do if you win the lottery

A winning Powerball or Mega Millions ticket is claimed from the lottery that sold it. Secure the ticket, file there before the deadline, and choose a payout under that lottery’s rules. Disclosure laws differ, and some have already changed. This is general information beside the Powerball results and Mega Millions results. It is not legal, tax, or financial advice. LotteryMigo cannot claim a prize or choose a payout. Statistics and generated picks do not guarantee a win, and this site is not responsible for any win or loss.

The first day

Match the ticket to the drawing it was bought for. Official numbers are on the selling lottery’s site and on powerball.com or megamillions.com. Results here copy the NY Open Data file and do not validate a ticket. A count on a Powerball number page or a Mega Millions number page does not change the next drawing, as the odds guide explains.

The paper ticket is the claim. Mega Millions calls an unsigned ticket a bearer instrument: anyone holding it can file, and a lost ticket is the player’s loss. Arizona says to sign the back and store it. New Mexico says to sign so only you can claim. Missouri says to sign first and lock the ticket away.

Signing in your own name can block an entity claim. Florida’s form uses the entity’s name, and its lawyers review the papers. Texas will not let you change how the prize is claimed after the ticket is validated and paid. California will not let a trust claim, so your name stays public. Arizona rejects a blind trust. Kansas pays one natural person, or a person named in writing, and the prize is not assignable. Ask a lawyer in the selling state before you sign, and keep an unsigned ticket locked up. Do not post the serial number or barcode.

Who to call, and who to ignore

Tell few people. Powerball says a lottery will not email or call about a prize unless you entered an official promotion, and it will not ask for a fee. The pattern is the scam guide. Call the number on the selling lottery’s site.

Hire a lawyer in that state, a tax professional, and a financial planner. Arizona points the cash-or-annuity question to those three and will not answer it. A caller who wants a cut to process the claim is not the lottery. LotteryMigo will not ask for a fee, a Social Security number, or a photo of the ticket.

Write an office pool down before anyone signs. Mega Millions allows a shared prize, with local limits. California requires every person on a group claim to elect the same payout.

Where the prize is claimed

Both games are sold by separate lotteries. You claim where the ticket was bought, even if you live elsewhere. Powerball does not require U.S. citizenship or residence in that state. Taxes can still apply. Matrices and prize tiers are in the Powerball and Mega Millions guides.

Powerball says a prize up to $600 can generally be paid by a retailer, and larger prizes go to an office or headquarters. Arizona retailers pay up to $100 and may pay up to $599. At $600 and above, use an office or the mail. New Mexico pays $1 to $600 at a retailer, $601 to $99,999 at headquarters or by mail, and $100,000 and up in person. Texas handles Powerball jackpots, Mega Millions jackpots, and annuities at headquarters in Austin, by appointment.

Claim deadlines

There is no national deadline. Powerball says a ticket usually expires in 90 days to one year, often printed on the back. Mega Millions counts that same range from the draw. An unclaimed jackpot returns to the participating lotteries in proportion to sales.

From current lottery pages and statutes: New Mexico drawing games, 90 days, or the next business day if the office is closed. Texas draw tickets, Florida draw prizes, District of Columbia Mega Millions, and Arizona draw tickets, 180 days. Ohio Revised Code § 3770.07 gives an online-game prize 180 days after it is announced. California allows 180 days on most draw games and one year on these jackpots. New York and Michigan allow one year from the draw. Confirm with the selling lottery. These windows move.

Cash or annuity

The advertised jackpot is an annuity beside a smaller cash figure, both before taxes. Powerball pays one sum now and 29 annual payments that rise 5 percent a year. The cash option is what must sit in the pool on drawing day to fund that annuity, and both numbers are estimates until sales close and the securities are priced. Mega Millions uses that schedule too, each later payment 5 percent larger. Shared tickets split the prize. Federal withholding, including the 24 percent rule, is on the lump-sum and annuity page. State tax follows the selling state.

Miss the election and you usually get the annuity. Florida wants the cash choice within 60 days after the draw. California gives 60 days from validation, then pays 30 installments if the form is late, and the choice cannot change. Kansas counts from validation, Missouri from the claim, and Arizona from the day the ticket is presented, each 60 days. Use the selling lottery’s form. If an annuitant dies, Powerball pays the estate and Mega Millions pays the beneficiary or the estate.

Names, trusts, and public records

Powerball says some states must release a winner’s name, city, game, and prize, and some accept a trust. The buyer must still be known to the lottery. The notes below are not every state. Virginia’s law changed in 2025 and 2026, and Florida’s 90-day name rule repeals on October 2, 2027 unless reenacted. Use the selling lottery’s claim page.

No dollar floor in the text cited, unless the winner agrees to publicity: Delaware withholds name and address (title 29, § 4818). Kansas withholds identity (K.S.A. § 74-8720) and still pays one natural person. Maryland withholds name and photo on draw games, FAST PLAY, and scratch-offs, not on second-chance promotions. Missouri (§ 313.303) withholds name and address unless the winner requests a form, which must say a signature is not required to be paid. New Jersey allows anonymity indefinitely. The claim is still reported for taxes and state debts. Oregon (House Bill 3115) withholds name and address. City, ZIP code, prize, and retailer stay public. Virginia (§ 58.1-4029) withholds an individual winner’s identity. Its 2025 amendment cut a line for prizes over $10 million to $1 million or greater. The sentence on the code site now states no dollar minimum.

A dollar line or a waiting period: Arizona (§ 5-573) keeps a name confidential on request at $100,000 or more, and for 90 days otherwise. City and county stay public. Georgia (§ 50-27-25) does so on written request at $250,000 or more. Minnesota keeps name and city private above $10,000 unless the winner opts in. Prize, game, and retailer stay public. At $10,000 or less, name and city are public. Texas (16 TAC § 401.324) allows the election at $1 million or more, including a beneficial owner, on claim day. An installment winner’s name may come out after 30 days. City, county, the entity, and its representative may be named, unless that representative is a beneficial owner who opted out. West Virginia (§ 29-22-15a) limits release of name, contact information, and likeness on a draw-game gross prize of $1 million or more. Courts and tax agencies can still receive it.

At $250,000 or more, Illinois (20 ILCS 1605/9) tells the director to withhold name and city if you ask at the claim. The same paragraph says a Freedom of Information Act request still prevails. California’s handbook makes full name, retailer, date, and amount public, and it will not let a trust claim. Florida (§ 24.1051) keeps address and phone confidential, and a name on $250,000 or more confidential for 90 days after the claim. It accepts an entity and still enforces the 180-day claim and the 60-day cash election. Ohio (§ 3770.07) makes an individual claimant’s name and address public. Trust beneficiaries’ names and Social Security numbers in the commission’s files stay confidential unless they consent. The state still collects them for debts.

A quiet claim leaves you known to the lottery, the IRS, and any offset. The largest jackpots were paid to named people, trusts, and companies. Confirm the current rule with the lottery that sold the ticket before you sign.
